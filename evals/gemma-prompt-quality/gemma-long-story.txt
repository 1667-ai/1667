Mara did not take the stair at once. The second bell had not struck, but the tower seemed to listen for it, and she had learned that listening things could make a person hurry merely by being still. She set Jun's lantern on the landing and opened its shutter. The wick was cold. A crust of salt lay in the cup where oil should have been, and in the salt someone had pressed the pad of a thumb. The print had filled with damp dust, then dried again. It was a small mark, ordinary as any mark made at a table, but it told her that Jun had stood here longer than a man would stand by accident. She looked down through the stairwell. The lower steps curved away into grey shadow. The rope at the door tapped the stone once, though no wind crossed the room. Mara closed the lantern shutter and kept the compass in her left hand. The brass was warm now, warm enough to feel almost alive.

At the next turn of the stair she found a row of nails driven into the wall. Each held a different thing: a blue button, a fish hook, a child's brass key, a strip of sailcloth, a spoon with its bowl worn thin. They were not trophies. They had been arranged by height, the smallest low and the larger objects above, as if somebody had begun a scale and then lost the rule for it. Beneath the nails, scratched into the stone with something blunt, were four lines of counting. The first three ended at nine. The fourth ended at two. Mara put her fingertip beside the final mark. The stone was damp there. A low pulse moved through the wall and into her nail, not sound exactly, but the memory of sound held in masonry. She withdrew her hand. In the marsh below, water brushed the tower foundations with a patient, flat noise.

She remembered Ivo at twelve, balancing spoons on the edge of the kitchen table while their mother mended a net by the fire. He had said that every object wanted to point somewhere if you gave it enough time. Their mother had told him not to be foolish. Ivo had smiled at Mara over the bright row of spoons, as if foolishness were a country he already knew how to enter. The thought came without permission, so clear that it made the tower disappear for a moment. Then the compass needle twitched, and the present returned with its wet dust and its waiting stair. Mara disliked the relief she felt. Memories were easier than doors. A door asked something back.

The door above the landing had no latch. It had been made from planks cut from different boats, and their old paint showed through the grey in narrow strips: red, green, a blue almost black with weather. A length of rope circled the boards twice and ended in a knot that Mara recognized from Jun's ferry. He taught it to children because it could be untied by someone who did not panic. Her hands did not shake until she tried the knot. Then the rope tightened under her fingers as if it had drawn breath. Mara stopped pulling. She set the compass against the wood. The needle swung toward the seam between two planks and stayed there.

"Jun," she said, quietly enough that it could have been a question or an apology. No answer came. There was only the far-off slap of the tide and the slight clink of the compass chain against its case. She worked the rope from the loose end, lifting rather than hauling. The knot yielded one grain at a time. When the final loop slipped free, the door opened inward by the width of a hand. A smell reached her: wet wool, old iron, and the sour sweetness of lamp smoke after rain. She did not push farther. Through the gap she saw a chair tipped against a table, one boot beneath the chair, and a line of black water moving across the floorboards in a direction no floor should have allowed.

The room had once been the bell keeper's office. A ledger lay open on the table, swollen by damp. Every page had been crossed out except the last. On it, a list of dates ran down the left side and a list of names down the right. The ink had bled until the names were mostly dark blots, but one remained legible: Ivo Vale. Mara read it twice because the first reading was not enough to make it real. Beside his name was a small circle, not a mark of completion but a mark she had seen on tide charts, meaning water over land. Her throat closed. She had imagined a hundred possible discoveries in the years since he vanished. None of them had included his name written in another person's careful hand.

She stepped inside. The black water withdrew from her boot and ran under the table as though ashamed of being seen. The boards gave a shallow groan. On the wall hung a map of the marsh, pinned at all four corners with rusted nails. The eastern channel had been drawn in red pencil, then erased so violently that the paper had torn. A new line, inked later, led from the tower to a blank white place beyond the salt flats. Jun's handwriting marked the line with three words: do not follow sound. Mara knew his blocky letters. He wrote like a man cutting kindling, every stroke useful and hard. She touched the edge of the map, but the paper curled away from her hand.

Something moved in the stairwell. Not a footstep. The sound was softer, a sleeve brushing a wall, followed by a pause that had intention in it. Mara lowered the lantern shutter again. The room became a thin shape around the window light. She picked up the boot from beneath the chair. It was Jun's. The leather at the heel had been patched with a strip of net, and the patch had been stitched in his uneven blue thread. The boot was dry. That frightened her more than the water did. Jun would not leave a boot. He complained when Mara forgot gloves. He measured every loss before he accepted it.

The second bell struck. The tower did not ring in answer. Instead, the water in the office rose in a single narrow column beside the table and held there, trembling. It reached Mara's wrist before it fell, cold enough to ache through bone. In the water's brief shape she saw a face turned away from her, a red scarf knotted at the throat. She caught at it. Her fingers closed on nothing. The column broke across the boards and slid beneath the door. From somewhere high above, a man coughed once. Mara stood very still, the compass pressing its pattern into her palm.

She followed the sound up another flight. The stair narrowed until her shoulders brushed both walls. Salt had grown in the mortar in white ridges, and every ridge caught the weak daylight from the windows. At one window she saw the marsh spread below her in grey squares, its channels laid out like broken glass. The ferry landing should have been visible from there. Instead she saw only a dark strip of water and a single upright pole, bare of boat or rope. Jun's ferry was gone. The sight did not prove anything, she told herself. Tides changed. Ropes broke. People made choices for reasons they did not explain. But the part of her that knew Jun had already begun to count absences.

At the top of the stair, the tower opened into the bell chamber. The bell hung above her, green-black with age, its mouth turned toward the floor. No rope ran from it. No beam held it. It seemed to rest in the air by an agreement the room had made with itself. Around the walls, old windows had lost their glass, and strips of cloud moved slowly through the openings. The air smelled of brine and crushed mint. A narrow bridge of planks crossed from the stair to the bell. On the far side stood a man in a dark coat, one hand against the bell's lip. He had Ivo's height, Ivo's way of leaning as if the world might move first. But his hair was cut short, and his face had the pale, unfinished look of someone who had not slept for a long time.

"Don't come closer," he said. The voice was Ivo's and not Ivo's. It had gained weight in places she did not know. Mara gripped the rail. "Where is Jun?" The man looked down at the floorboards rather than at her. "Below, if below still means anything." His answer angered her because it was a riddle and because part of her wanted to understand it. "You don't get to speak like that," she said. "You left." He flinched at the plainness of it. For a moment he was her brother again, caught with a lie too small to survive. Then the bell above them gave a soundless shiver, and he turned toward it as if it had called his name.

Ivo told her the bell did not pull people under the water. It pulled the water out of its place. Each time it rang, the marsh remembered a different shoreline. Paths appeared where channels had been. Houses stood for an hour on drowned ground. The lost could return, but only if someone kept the return open from the tower. He had tried to leave after the first year. The path had led him back to the bell chamber with three days missing from his body and none from his mind. Jun had found him two weeks ago at the eastern channel, half frozen and carrying a length of red scarf. Jun had come to the tower because he believed a ferry rope could hold what a bell rope could not.

Mara did not believe the story, not wholly. Belief was too generous a word for the way the room tilted around it. But she believed the details. Jun would have tried a rope. Ivo would have stayed near a mechanism he did not understand if he thought it could be fixed. The damp ledger, the map, the boot, all of them fitted the story with the cruel precision of teeth in a key. She asked where the rope was. Ivo pointed beneath the bridge. Through the gaps she saw it descending past the bell's shadow into the hollow center of the tower. It had been tied around the clapper. Each strand was dark with water, and every few seconds it trembled as though something below had tugged it.

"If the third bell rings," Ivo said, "the rope will go slack." Mara looked at the windows. The sky had changed from pewter to the colour of old bone. Moonrise was still a pale idea beyond the flats, but the tide was coming in. She could smell it now, the iron-rich water climbing the channels. "Then we pull Jun up before it rings." Ivo laughed once, without humor. "That is what I said." He lifted his hand from the bell. His palm was blistered and stained green. "It takes two people to keep the rope tight. The one at the bell cannot leave it. The one below cannot see what is pulling. And the bell has been teaching the water our names."

Mara crossed the bridge. The planks bowed under her, but did not break. Up close, the bell's surface was covered in small words scratched by different hands. Some were names. Some were dates. One was a recipe for eel broth. One said simply, I was here and I am sorry. The ordinary messages made her chest hurt more than the names did. People had waited here long enough to become bored. They had been cold, hungry, frightened, and still they had left proof of themselves in the only place they could reach. Mara put her palm beside Ivo's. The bronze was colder than the water downstairs.

The third bell began before either of them moved. It did not come as a strike. It came as pressure behind Mara's eyes, a darkening at the edges of the room. The rope snapped taut. Ivo caught it with both hands and was pulled forward until his shoulder struck the bell. Mara seized the other length. The fibers burned her palms. Below them, something heavy dragged through water. The tower leaned toward the eastern marsh. Through the open windows Mara saw channels straighten into roads, then curl back into water. A house appeared on the flats, its roof bright with rain. A woman stood in its doorway holding a basin, though no one had lived there for fifty years.

"Jun!" Mara shouted. The rope answered with three hard pulls. Ivo's face changed. "He heard you." Together they hauled. The line rose an inch, then another. It was not only Jun's weight. It felt as if the rope had caught the whole marsh by its hidden roots. Mara planted her boots against the bridge rail and pulled until her shoulders shook. Ivo matched her, breath by breath. For a time there was no past and no explanation, only the rough hemp, the wet air, and the choice to keep hold. The bell's pressure increased. Blood ran from the split skin of Mara's right hand and darkened the rope.

Something surfaced in the bell's shadow. A hand appeared first, pale against the black water, then a sleeve, then Jun's face. He was not below the tower. He was in the hollow where the bell should have had foundations, suspended in a column of water that did not fall. His eyes were open. He had a knife between his teeth and one end of the ferry rope wound around his chest. When he saw Mara, he bit down hard enough that the knife handle shook. He raised two fingers, not in greeting but in command: wait. Mara hated him for it. She hated that he was alive and still able to tell her what to do.

The water column widened. Shapes moved behind Jun, the outlines of people walking slowly through a street that was no longer there. Mara saw a woman in a yellow coat, a man carrying a suitcase, a child with a paper crown. None looked at her. Their faces were calm, turned toward a direction beyond the tower. Ivo whispered that they were the ones who had followed the bell. Jun had tied himself to the rope so he would not follow them. Mara understood then why the rope was not enough. It held a body, but the bell called at the part of a person that made a body choose where to go.

Jun pulled the knife from his mouth and cut at something below the waterline. The rope lurched. Ivo cried out and nearly lost his grip. Mara reached farther, wrapping the line around her forearm. The burn became a clean line of pain. The bell chamber filled with wind. Loose papers rose from the office far below and spun up the stairwell like pale birds. One struck Mara's cheek. She saw the ledger's last page before it flew away: Ivo Vale, circle; Jun Arlen, circle; Mara Vale, blank. The blank was worse than the circles. It meant the tower had been waiting for her.

She spoke her own name aloud. The sound seemed too small in the chamber. Then she said it again, not to answer the bell but to keep it from becoming a thing the bell could say for her. Ivo stared at her. Jun's eyes closed briefly, whether in pain or relief she could not tell. Mara leaned her weight backward. The rope rose another handspan. Water spilled over the bridge and ran around her boots. It carried bits of reed, a silver button, a bone-white fish spine. In the water she saw the reflection of the bell, but the reflection showed it cracked open from crown to mouth.

The crack was real a moment later. A thin green line raced down the bronze. The sound that followed was not loud. It was intimate, like a cup breaking in an empty kitchen. Ivo let go of the bell with one hand. Mara shouted at him, but he pressed his bleeding palm into the crack. The bell stopped pulling for one breath. That breath was enough. Jun came through the water column feet first, struck the bridge, and rolled against the rail. Mara dropped the rope and caught his coat. He was heavy, solid, furious with cold. His mouth opened, but what he said was lost under the sudden roar of water falling where water had no right to fall.

The tower began to come apart. Not all at once. The stones shifted in the careful, offended way of old walls that had carried too much for too long. Ivo still had his hand on the bell. Mara dragged Jun toward the stair. He tried to stand and failed, then grabbed the rail and hauled himself upright with a curse. "Leave it," he said to Ivo. Ivo shook his head. The red scarf at his throat had come loose. Its end lifted in the wind and pointed east. Mara saw, beyond the open windows, a road of black water unrolling across the flats. On it stood a dozen figures, then a hundred, all facing the tower.

"We can come back," Mara said. It was a lie, but it was the lie she needed. Ivo looked at her with the exhausted patience of someone who had lived beside lies for years. "You can," he said. "That is the difference." The bell crack widened beneath his hand. A note rose from it, high and thin. Jun pushed Mara toward the stair. She resisted until the bridge bucked under them. Then she ran, not because she agreed, but because the tower made every decision smaller than survival.

They descended through dust and salt. The office door had fallen open. Water poured across the floor toward the stairwell, carrying the map, the boot, and the row of small objects from the nails. Mara saw the child's key spin past her ankle. She caught it without knowing why. Jun kept one hand on the wall and the other on her shoulder whenever the steps shook. He was bleeding from the forehead. Each time she tried to ask about Ivo, he said, "Down first." His voice had become a rope of its own, something to hold until there was ground.

At the tower door, the marsh had changed. The stones Mara had crossed were gone. In their place, a strip of black water led east through reeds that bent away from it. The ferry pole stood in the distance, but Jun's boat was tied to it upside down, its hull full of moonlight. Behind them, the bell chamber groaned. Mara turned. Ivo stood in the top window, one hand raised. The red scarf streamed from his wrist. For an instant the figure looked like a boy on a kitchen chair, balancing a spoon and waiting for her to laugh. Then the tower wall folded inward, and the window was full of water.

Jun pulled Mara into the reeds before she could go back. She fought him once, then twice, then stopped because the ground beneath her feet had begun to soften. The eastern road called with the sound of many distant bells. Jun said nothing about it. He guided her along a path made of broken shell and drowned grass, taking turns she could not see until they were beneath them. The compass in Mara's hand pointed nowhere. Its needle spun, slowed, and finally settled toward the child’s key she had taken from the water. She closed her fingers around both pieces of brass and followed Jun into the darkening marsh.

They reached the ferry pole after the last colour had gone from the west. The upside-down boat had righted itself, but it lay half full of water and reed fragments, rocking against its rope with the stubborn irritation of an animal that had been woken. Jun bailed with his hat. Mara used both hands because the burn on her arm had stiffened and because doing a small task kept the tower from filling every space in her mind. They worked without speaking. The water they threw overboard struck the marsh with dull sounds. Each splash seemed to travel too far. Once, Mara looked toward the flats and saw the tower standing whole against the sky. When she blinked, it was only a broken black shape, empty at the top.

Jun found the oars beneath the bench. One had split near the blade. He wrapped the break with the strip of sailcloth from the tower office, pulling the knot with his teeth while Mara held the wood steady. "You should not have come after me," he said at last. Mara watched his hands. They were shaking now that there was time for them to shake. "You left your boat," she said. "I was offended." A laugh tried to rise in him and failed. He pressed his thumb against the bandage at his forehead. "That is a poor reason to cross a marsh at bell tide." "It got me here." Jun looked toward the tower. His face closed, then opened again with effort. "It got you here this time. I do not know what it will cost next time."

Mara put the child's key on the bench between them. In the moonlight it looked almost white. Jun did not touch it. He said there had been a house at the eastern channel when he first found Ivo, a house with a red door and smoke coming from the chimney. He had knocked. A woman had answered, but when she saw the bell keeper's tower behind him she shut the door without a word. When Jun turned back, the house was gone and the key was in his pocket. He had kept it because he did not know whose it was. Mara closed her hand around the key. The metal was still warm from her skin. She wondered how many objects the marsh had returned to the wrong people, and whether returning was still a kindness when it came with a price.

They pushed the boat from the reeds. The ferry rope pulled tight, leading them west toward the familiar bank. Mara sat in the bow with the compass and the key wrapped in her scarf. Behind her, Jun rowed slowly, listening to the water as if it might speak in a voice he could recognize. The bell did not ring again that night. That silence did not comfort her. It felt like a hand held above a table, waiting to choose where to fall. As the boat crossed the first channel, Mara saw a red thread caught on the reeds. She reached for it, but it drifted away before her fingers touched it. She let it go. The moon rose over Greywater Reach, thin and cold, and the boat moved on beneath it.

Near the western bank, Jun stopped rowing. The ferry rope had gone slack, though the current still pulled under the boat. He lifted the oar and listened. Mara heard nothing at first. Then, far beneath the ordinary sounds of water and wood, came the faint scrape of metal on stone. It was not a bell. It was smaller, closer to the turning of a key in a lock. The compass needle moved toward the marsh behind them, then toward the key in her hand. Mara did not tell Jun. Some knowledge needed a night to settle before it became speech. She watched the bank approach: the low shed, the piled nets, the lamp burning in the window of the empty ferry house. Everything looked returned to itself, but she knew now that return was not the same as safety.

When the boat touched mud, Jun stepped out first and held it steady while Mara climbed onto the bank. His coat dripped onto the grass. He looked older than he had at dusk. Mara almost asked him to promise that he would not go back to the tower. Instead she took the lantern from his hand and trimmed its wick. A small flame gathered, imperfect but steady. Together they walked toward the ferry house without looking east. Behind them, the black water closed over the boat's wake. In Mara's pocket, the child’s key touched the compass with each step, a quiet, repeated sound that was not yet an answer.
